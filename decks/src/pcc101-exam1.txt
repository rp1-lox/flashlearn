PCC (definition)	Applied chemistry of the composition, structure, properties, transformations, and applications of polymers and colorants (dyes and pigments)
5 branches of chemistry + PCC example	Organic: azo dye synthesis. Inorganic: metal ions (copper) for wash fastness. Analytical: HPLC dye purity. Physical: Tg, chain mobility. Biochemistry: drug-delivery polymers
Main branch PCC falls under	Organic chemistry (carbon-based)
Atomic number =	Number of protons
Neutrons =	Mass (rounded to a whole number) minus protons
Electrons in a neutral atom =	Number of protons
17 protons, mass 35	Chlorine. 18 neutrons, 17 electrons
Aluminum (Z = 13, mass 26.98)	13 protons, 14 neutrons, 13 electrons
Molecule	2 or more atoms bonded together (O2, H2O)
Compound	Atoms of 2 or more DIFFERENT elements bonded together
NaCl: molecule or compound?	Compound, NOT a molecule (ionic lattice, no discrete unit)
O2: molecule or compound?	Molecule, NOT a compound (one element)
CO2: best term?	Compound (it is also a molecule)
Dye: molecule or compound?	Both (discrete covalent unit, several elements)
Shell capacities (this class)	2, 8, 8
Valence electrons (groups 13 to 18)	Group number minus 10
Valence electrons: C, N, O, Cl, H	C 4, N 5, O 6, Cl 7, H 1
Inert elements	Noble gases: full valence shell (octet; He has 2). Do not bond
Reactive elements	Incomplete valence shell: alkali metals (Na), halogens (Cl, F)
Most likely to bond: He, Ne, Na, Ar?	Na: 1 valence electron, shell far from full
Octet rule	Atoms bond to reach 8 valence electrons (H and He: 2)
Primary bonding	Chemical bonds, strong: ionic and covalent
Secondary bonding	Physical interactions, weak: hydrogen bonds, van der Waals. Wash out easily
Ionic bond	Electrons TRANSFERRED; metal + nonmetal; e.g. NaCl
Covalent bond	Electrons SHARED; e.g. C-H, polymer backbones
PCC ionic bond example	Acid dye (Acid Orange 7) bonds to nylon's protonated amine end (-NH3+)
Electronegativity trend	Increases UP and to the RIGHT (toward fluorine). F highest, metals lowest
Rank F, O, C, Na by electronegativity	F > O > C > Na
Carbon bonds and lone pairs	Always 4 bonds, no lone pairs
Oxygen lone pairs	2 (two bonds + two lone pairs)
Nitrogen lone pairs	1 (three bonds + one lone pair)
Water Lewis structure	O in center, 2 H, 2 lone pairs on O
Ammonia Lewis structure	N in center, 3 H, 1 lone pair on N
O2 Lewis structure	O=O double bond, 2 lone pairs on each O (4 total)
Skeletal structure rule	A carbon at every corner and line end. Hydrogens hidden: each C gets enough H to make 4 bonds. O and N written out
Carbons in a zigzag of n line segments	n + 1 (count the points, not the lines)
Hidden hydrogens on a benzene ring	Each ring carbon with nothing attached has 1 hidden H
Benzene formula	C6H6
Benzene with -OH (phenol)	C6H6O (5 ring H + 1 on the O)
Benzene with -CH3 (toluene)	C7H8
Benzene can be drawn 3 ways	Alternating single/double bonds; same with double bonds shifted (resonance); hexagon with a circle inside
Alkane	All single C-C bonds; saturated; e.g. ethane C2H6
Alkene	At least one C=C double bond; unsaturated; e.g. ethylene C2H4
Alkyne	C≡C triple bond; unsaturated; e.g. acetylene C2H2
Saturated vs unsaturated	Saturated = all single bonds. Unsaturated = has a double or triple bond
Macromolecular Hypothesis	Polymers are long chains of repeat units held together by COVALENT bonds (Staudinger, Nobel Prize)
What people believed before Staudinger	Polymers were clumps of small molecules held by weak forces (secondary bonding)
Staudinger equation	Shown in class; NOT to memorize
Macromolecule	A molecule with a very large number of atoms (high molecular weight): proteins, carbohydrates, nucleic acids, polymers
Monomer	1 unit; the small molecule that links up
Oligomer	2 to 10 repeat units
Polymer	Thousands of repeat units, covalently bonded
Repeat unit	The piece inside the brackets that repeats n times
Addition monomer template	H2C=CHR (only R changes)
Ethylene	H2C=CH2 (R = H) -> polyethylene (PE)
Propylene	H2C=CH-CH3 (R = CH3) -> polypropylene (PP)
Styrene	H2C=CH-benzene (R = benzene ring) -> polystyrene (PS, Styrofoam)
Vinyl chloride	H2C=CHCl (R = Cl) -> polyvinyl chloride (PVC)
PE repeat unit	-[CH2-CH2]n-  (C2H4, 28.05 g/mol)
PP repeat unit	-[CH2-CH(CH3)]n-  (C3H6, 42.08 g/mol)
PS repeat unit	-[CH2-CH(C6H5)]n-  (C8H8, 104.14 g/mol)
PVC repeat unit	-[CH2-CHCl]n-
What happens to the C=C in addition polymerization	The double bond opens; its electrons form single bonds to neighboring monomers (the backbone)
Addition vs condensation polymerization	Addition: double bond opens, nothing lost. Condensation: a small molecule (water) is lost at each link
Nylon-6,6 monomers	1,6-diaminohexane (H2N-(CH2)6-NH2) + adipic acid (HOOC-(CH2)4-COOH). Both have 6 carbons
Nylon-6,6 link	Amide, C(=O)-NH. Water lost (condensation)
Nylon-6,6 repeat unit	-[NH-(CH2)6-NH-C(=O)-(CH2)4-C(=O)]-  C12H22N2O2, 226.32 g/mol
What does "6,6" mean	Both monomers have 6 carbons
PET monomers	Terephthalic acid (benzene ring with COOH on opposite ends) + ethylene glycol (HO-CH2-CH2-OH)
PET link	Ester, C(=O)-O. Water lost (condensation)
PET repeat unit	-[O-CH2-CH2-O-C(=O)-C6H4-C(=O)]-  C10H8O4, 192.16 g/mol
Build any condensation polymer	Acid + amine (or alcohol): remove OH from acid and H from the other, join the ends
Nylon-6,6: homopolymer or heteropolymer?	Homopolymer: the fused diamine+diacid unit is one repeat unit that repeats identically
6 classification categories	Origin, monomer composition, chain architecture, monomer arrangement, material behavior, crystallinity
Origin classes	Natural, synthetic, semi-synthetic (modified)
Natural polymer examples	Cellulose (cotton), proteins
Synthetic polymer examples	Polyethylene, nylon-6,6, PET
Semi-synthetic examples	Rayon, cellulose acetate / triacetate (chemically modified cellulose)
Homopolymer	One type of repeat unit
Heteropolymer	Two or more different repeat units (includes all copolymers)
Protein classification	Heteropolymer and biopolymer (amino acids vary)
4 copolymer arrangements	Random (ABAABB), alternating (ABAB), block (AAABBB), graft (A backbone with B side chains)
Chain architecture classes	Linear, branched, cross-linked, network
Thermoplastic	Softens when heated, hardens when cooled; can re-melt and recycle
Thermoset	Cross-linked; will not re-melt
Elastomer	Stretches and snaps back (rubbery)
Crystalline region	Chains ordered and tightly packed
Amorphous region	Chains disordered
Semi-crystalline	Has both crystalline and amorphous regions (most polymers)
HDPE vs LDPE	HDPE: linear, packs tightly, more crystalline, stiff (milk jug). LDPE: branched, packs poorly, less crystalline, floppy (plastic bag)
Cross-linked polymer in base (Lab 1 poly(acrylic acid))	Swells but does not dissolve; chains tied together
Repeat-unit molar mass	Add atomic masses of atoms INSIDE the brackets only (not end groups)
Atomic masses (C, H, N, O, Cl)	C 12.01, H 1.008, N 14.01, O 16.00, Cl 35.45
Degree of polymerization (DP)	DP = polymer molar mass / repeat-unit molar mass. Round to a whole number
Polymer molar mass from DP	Molar mass = DP x repeat-unit molar mass
PE sample 50,000 g/mol: DP?	50000 / 28.05 = 1783
Tg (glass transition temperature)	Polymer goes from rigid/glassy to soft/rubbery. Governs AMORPHOUS regions
Tm (melting temperature)	Crystalline regions melt and lose order. Governs CRYSTALLINE regions
Tg spaghetti analogy	Dry spaghetti = below Tg (rigid). Cooked spaghetti = above Tg (flexible)
Polymer with Tg but no Tm	A fully amorphous polymer, e.g. polystyrene
Why dye polyester above Tg	Amorphous chains become mobile and open up so dye can get in
Free-radical polymerization mechanism	NOT on the exam
